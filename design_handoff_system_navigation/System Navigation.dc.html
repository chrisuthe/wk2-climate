What we're replacing

Roughly 24 targets in three rows of 227 px. Nothing is bigger than about 70 px tall. Everything is the same weight, the same chrome-embossed grey, and the same size, so nothing reads first — the two temperatures you actually change are the same visual weight as the rear seat heater you touch twice a year.

Design rules I'm working to
  1. Six things in the bar, everything else one tap away. Driver temp, passenger temp, AUTO, heated wheel, volume, home/back. That's your list, and it's a third of what's there now.
  2. 96 px minimum, and most targets much larger. Fewer controls is what buys the size — this is arithmetic, not styling.
  3. Direct selection, never cycling. Commands 8/9/10/11 are idempotent airflow setters, so four lit buttons replace the OEM four-step cycle.
  4. State visible without reading. Active = filled and bright; inactive = outline. Warm/cool is carried in colour and fill position, so a glance is enough.
  5. Rest at 227, expand on demand. Shape C. com.syu.air keeps running underneath, so the inset survives and a bug in this bar never leaves the car without climate control.
  6. Two themes. Night is the default; day raises luminance without changing layout, so muscle memory holds.
Where the research lands

The direction of travel in production cars is away from what this unit does. Euro NCAP starts penalising touchscreen-only essential controls in 2026; comparison testing has task times going from about 10 seconds on a physical-knob Volvo to 23–30 seconds on Tesla and BMW touch UIs. You have no physical HVAC controls to fall back on, so the touch UI has to do the work of a knob: large targets in fixed positions, absolute rather than relative input, and no menu to enter. That is why every option below keeps both temperatures permanently on screen and never nests them.

Icons here are deliberately primitive placeholders and the text labels are doing the work. Once you pick a direction I'd swap in a real icon set — or keep the labels, which for a driver is arguably the better answer.

94°F · HOT — SLOT FLIPS TO SEAT COOL
The rule: outside temperature can change what one 200 × 96 slot contains, and nothing else. It holds the seat control you’re actually going to want — seat heat when it’s cool out, seat cooling when it’s hot — and yields to front defrost below freezing, where clearing the glass outranks comfort. Every other control stays at the same coordinates at 28°F and 94°F, so nothing you reach for by feel ever moves. That's the constraint that makes this safe rather than clever — a bar that rearranges itself is a bar you have to read every time.
Reading outside temp is free: it's already on the bus you're subscribed to, alongside the U_AIR_* codes. The bar no longer prints the number — that lives elsewhere on screen — it just spends it, and the reclaimed width goes to the heated wheel as a permanent control. Note this also fixed 1a's one real flaw — its top pills were 56 px tall, under your 96 px floor. Here that whole row is 96 px.
Two things to decide: whether the slot ever hands you a control that exists nowhere else in the bar (I say no — it must always be a shortcut, never the only route), and whether the slot should also react to what the vehicle is doing rather than just ambient temperature, e.g. defrost promoted when the windshield is actually fogging rather than at a fixed 32°F threshold.
